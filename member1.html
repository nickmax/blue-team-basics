Why this matters

Most breaches start with a message

Phishing is not a technical attack. It is a conversation with a deadline. Most messages that steal credentials never touch code — they borrow a logo, copy a tone, and ask you to do one small thing quickly. The four cards below collect the red flags we see most often at university and at work.

Read them once, then use the three-step check before you click anything that asks for a password, a payment, or a file. If a message feels urgent, that is the moment to slow down.

01 · The tells

Four red flags

Urgency

Borrowed urgency

Payroll closes in ten minutes. Your account will be suspended tonight. Real departments give you days, not minutes, and they never threaten you into clicking.

Authority

A uniform is not proof

A familiar logo, a job title and a calm tone are cheap to fake. Treat any request for a password or a code as suspicious, even when the sender looks official.

Lookalike

Links that almost match

rit.edu and rit-edu.support are different addresses. Hover over a link, read the domain from right to left, and type the address yourself when in doubt.

Attachment

Files you did not ask for

An unexpected invoice, CV or “scanned document” can carry malware. If you were not expecting the file, confirm with the sender through a channel you already trust.

02 · The habit

The three-step check

  1. Stop the clock

    Do not reply, click or call the number in the message. Urgency is the attacker’s main tool, so refusing to hurry removes it.

  2. Check the channel

    Contact the person or office through the phone number or website you already use — never the contact details printed in the message.

  3. Report, then delete

    Forward the message to your IT or security team, then delete it. Reporting one phishing email protects everyone else on the same list.

Hear the pressure tactic

This simulated voicemail uses every trick in the guide: a friendly name, a deadline, a threat to your course access and a request for a one-time code. It was recorded for this class; no real service desk is involved.

Read the transcript

“Hi, this is Daniel from the IT Service Desk. We are migrating student accounts to the new portal tonight, and yours has not been verified yet. If it is not confirmed in the next thirty minutes, your email and course materials will be suspended. Open the link in this message, and sign in with your university password so we can keep your access active. If you have any trouble, just reply with the six digit code you receive.”

Notice what is missing: no ticket number, no official domain and no reason to trust the caller. The pressure is the point.
